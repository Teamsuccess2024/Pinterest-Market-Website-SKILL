**Intake Questionnaire: The Questions To Ask Before Building Anything**

**Why This File Exists**

This is the first template in the skill, and it sits at the very start of the workflow. Before keyword research, before profile setup, before any board is named or any pin designed, the operator needs to understand the situation. Who is the user? What do they sell? Who do they serve? What do they have to work with? What does success look like for them?

Most Pinterest engagements skip this step, or do a sloppy version of it, and pay for that shortcut for months afterward. The user explains their business in two sentences, the operator nods, and they jump straight into building. Three months later it becomes clear that the keyword research was aimed at the wrong audience, the boards don't match the actual content inventory, the destinations aren't built to convert the traffic that's arriving, and the goals never got clarified, so nobody knows whether the work is succeeding. The whole engagement has to be redone or quietly abandoned.

The intake questionnaire prevents this. It forces a structured conversation upfront, captures the answers in writing, and produces a foundation document that every other file in the skill can reference. The keyword research file uses these answers to know what audience to research for. The board strategy file uses them to know what content the boards need to feed. The pin design file uses them to know what brand aesthetic to align with. The conversion file uses them to know what the destination pages need to deliver. None of those files can do their work properly without the intake's output.

The good news is that the intake doesn't have to be exhaustive. Twenty to thirty minutes of focused conversation, captured in writing, is enough for almost every engagement. The bad news is that most users want to skip it because it feels like preliminary work that delays the "real" work. The skill is written specifically to push back on that impulse. The intake _is_ the real work. Everything that follows depends on it.

This file lays out the questions to ask, the order to ask them in, what to do when the user doesn't have answers yet, how to capture the output, and how the output then feeds into the rest of the skill. It is structured as a working document - the operator can read through the questions, ask them in order, and capture answers in the final compiled format at the end.

**The Spirit Of The Conversation**

A few principles to carry into the intake conversation, before getting into the questions themselves.

**Get specific, not abstract.** Users will naturally answer questions in vague, aspirational terms. "We help people grow in their faith." "We want to drive traffic to our site." "Our audience is Christians." None of these answers are useful for building a Pinterest strategy. The job of the intake is to push past the abstractions to the specifics. _Which_ people? _How much_ traffic? _Which_ Christians? Specific answers produce specific strategies. Abstract answers produce abstract strategies that don't work.

**Don't accept "I don't know" as the final answer.** Users sometimes say "I don't know" to questions when they actually do know but aren't used to articulating it. A follow-up like "best guess" or "what does your gut tell you" almost always surfaces a real answer. Sometimes the real answer is genuinely "I don't know yet, this is something we need to figure out" - and that's fine, because then the operator knows that part of the engagement is going to involve helping the user figure it out. But it should never end with "I don't know" without a follow-up.

**Capture answers in the user's own words when possible.** When the user gives a particularly clear or specific answer, write it down verbatim rather than paraphrasing. The user's own language is what should show up in pin copy, board descriptions, and the brand voice across the entire account. Paraphrasing into operator-speak loses the texture that makes the brand specific.

**Take notes during the conversation, then compile afterward.** During the conversation, scribble notes. After the conversation, compile the notes into the structured output format at the end of this file. This separation prevents the conversation from feeling like a form-filling exercise and lets the user speak naturally.

**Use the conversation to detect mismatches.** Sometimes the intake reveals that Pinterest isn't actually a good fit for the user. Their audience isn't on Pinterest. Their content doesn't multiplied well. Their goals require faster feedback than Pinterest can provide. Catching mismatches at the intake stage and being honest about them is far better than spending three months building something that won't work. Most of the time, Pinterest is a good fit and the intake confirms it. Occasionally it isn't, and the intake saves everyone a lot of wasted effort.

**Section One: The Business Context**

The first set of questions establishes who the user is and what they're trying to accomplish at the broadest level. These questions might feel basic - "tell me about your business" - but the specifics matter, and a few minutes here saves significant time later.

**Question 1: What is the website, and what does it offer?**

The user gives the URL and a one-paragraph description of what's on the site. Push for specifics about what kinds of content or products live there. "A Christian library" is not enough. "TeamSuccessNetwork.com - a free Christian library offering thirty-plus books on biblical leadership, devotional content, and Christian living, with new books added monthly. Most books are free downloads in exchange for an email signup; some future books will be paid." That's enough.

**Question 2: How long has the website existed, and what's its current traffic situation?**

The history matters. A brand-new site with no existing traffic has different needs than a five-year-old site with established SEO. A site that already gets significant traffic from another channel (organic search, email, paid ads) has different conversion expectations than a site starting from scratch. Capture: how long the site has existed, what its current monthly visitors look like, and where the existing traffic comes from.

**Question 3: What is the website's primary purpose?**

Different sites have different primary purposes, and the Pinterest strategy must align. Possible answers: building an email list, selling products directly, generating ad revenue from page views, generating leads for a service business, building brand awareness, supporting a community. Sometimes the user has multiple purposes - that's fine, but ask which is most important right now. The Pinterest strategy will be designed to optimize the most important purpose first.

**Question 4: Is there an existing Pinterest presence, and if so, what's its current state?**

Many users have a Pinterest account they started years ago, pinned to a few times, and forgot about. Some have an active account that's underperforming. Some are starting completely fresh. The intake needs to know which scenario applies.

If there's an existing account, ask for the URL or username so the operator can review it. Look at: how many boards exist, how many pins per board, what the profile copy looks like, whether the account is Business or personal, whether the website is verified, whether Rich Pins are active, what the current monthly impressions and outbound clicks look like. This audit alone often surfaces several of the mistakes from the common-mistakes file, which become immediate priorities.

If there's no existing presence, the engagement will be greenfield - and that's actually slightly easier, since there's no inherited mess to clean up.

**Question 5: What's the budget - both money and time?**

Pinterest is a low-cost channel, but it's not zero-cost in either money or time. The user needs to know what they're committing to. Ask:

How many hours per week can be dedicated to Pinterest? (For most accounts, two to four hours per week is the sustainable target.)

Is there budget for tools (Tailwind subscription, Canva Pro, etc.)? (Optional but useful at scale.)

Is there budget for production help (a virtual assistant, a designer)? (Not required, but accelerates growth significantly when present.)

How long is the user committing to before they evaluate whether to continue? (Should be at least twelve months - Pinterest's ramp is real.)

Capture honest answers, not aspirational ones. A user who says they'll dedicate ten hours per week and actually has time for two will quietly fail. Better to plan around the realistic two hours than aspire to the unrealistic ten.

**Section Two: The Audience**

The second set of questions develops a clear picture of who the user is trying to reach. This section often takes the most time in the conversation, because users frequently haven't thought about their audience in this much specificity. The questions are designed to push past surface-level descriptions to the texture of who real audience members are.

**Question 6: Who is the primary audience for the website?**

Not "who could possibly benefit" - who is the _primary_ target. Force a single sentence answer. "Christian pastors and ministry leaders looking for biblical resources to support their teaching and personal growth." Or "Christian working moms looking for practical devotional content that fits a busy schedule." Or "New believers exploring foundational Christian living and seeking practical guidance for their first year of faith." Specificity matters.

**Question 7: What does this audience search for on Pinterest?**

Most users have never thought about this. Push them to imagine the audience sitting down at Pinterest with a problem. What would they type into the search bar? Examples for a Christian audience might include: "Bible verses for anxious mornings," "Christian books for new believers," "daily devotionals for women," "biblical leadership principles," "scripture for working moms." If the user can come up with five to ten phrases their audience would search, that's a strong starting point for the keyword research file. If they can't come up with any, that's a signal that more keyword discovery work will be needed in the keyword research step.

**Question 8: What problem is the audience trying to solve?**

People go to Pinterest with intent - they're looking for help with something specific. Ask the user: what is the problem the audience is trying to solve when they search for content like this? Possible answers might be: "They want to feel closer to God in the morning before their day gets chaotic." "They're trying to find leadership wisdom that aligns with their faith." "They're new to Christianity and overwhelmed by where to start." Naming the problem clarifies what the destination pages need to deliver and what the pin copy needs to promise.

**Question 9: What does the audience value, dislike, and respond to?**

A few minutes spent on the texture of the audience pays dividends across every other file. Ask:

What does this audience genuinely value? (For Christian audiences, this might be: scripture grounding, theological seriousness, warmth, accessibility, practical application.)

What turns this audience off? (Might be: prosperity gospel, shallow inspiration, sales pressure, language that feels too churchy, language that feels too secular.)

What aesthetic does this audience respond to? (Might be: warm and pastoral, dark and reverent, bright and joyful, minimalist and thoughtful.)

Capture these in the user's own words. They feed directly into pin design choices, copy voice, and brand alignment throughout the rest of the work.

**Question 10: Are there secondary audiences worth considering?**

Many sites serve multiple audiences. The Christian library might primarily target pastors but also serve working moms, new believers, and Christian business leaders. Each secondary audience can become its own pin angle, board, and content stream. Capture the secondary audiences explicitly so the keyword research and board architecture can include them.

**Section Three: The Content Inventory**

The third set of questions establishes what the user has to work with. Pinterest production multiplies what already exists - it doesn't conjure content from nothing. Knowing the inventory upfront prevents the awkward situation where the strategy assumes content that doesn't exist or ignores content that does.

**Question 11: What content currently exists on the website?**

Get a count and a categorization. "Twenty-three published books, with twelve more in active production. Books are roughly grouped into: biblical leadership (six titles), devotional content (eight titles), Christian identity (four titles), spiritual warfare (three titles), faith and suffering (two titles)." Or whatever the actual breakdown is.

If the user has an unstructured pile of content, take the time to help them categorize it. The categorization will roughly map to the board structure that gets built later.

**Question 12: What's the production rate of new content?**

Is the user publishing one new piece of content per month? Per week? Per quarter? The pace of new content affects the Pinterest strategy in two ways: it determines how often new pin sets need to be produced, and it determines how heavily the strategy needs to lean on refresh waves of existing content versus new launches.

For Team Success Network, the user's rate of book production is high - multiple books per month. This is favorable for Pinterest because it means a steady stream of new launch opportunities. A site that publishes one piece of content per quarter would need to lean much more heavily on refresh waves and multi-pin variants per piece.

**Question 13: What content is the user most proud of, and what's been most successful elsewhere?**

These are the breakout candidates for Pinterest. If a particular book has been the user's most-downloaded, that book deserves disproportionate Pinterest investment. If a particular blog post has gotten unusual traction on email or SEO, that post is likely to also resonate on Pinterest. The content that has already proven itself in other channels is the safest bet for high-leverage Pinterest production.

Ask the user to name three to five pieces of content that fit this description, and write down what made each one resonate. The patterns will inform the Pinterest production priorities.

**Question 14: Is there content that should not be promoted on Pinterest?**

Some content is great but not Pinterest-suited. Highly technical content for a narrow professional audience might not have a Pinterest audience. Content that requires significant context (later chapters of a book series, advanced theological work) might confuse cold Pinterest visitors. Time-sensitive content (event recaps, news commentary) doesn't multiply well.

Knowing what's off-limits prevents wasted production effort and helps the user understand that Pinterest is for the evergreen, accessible, foundational content - not necessarily everything they've published.

**Question 15: What content is planned but not yet produced, and could be informed by Pinterest demand?**

This is a powerful question that most users haven't considered. If the user is planning to write a new book, and Pinterest keyword research reveals that "Christian books on overcoming anxiety" is a high-volume search with relatively low competition, that data could inform the new book's positioning, title, and content. Pinterest doesn't just multiply existing content - it can shape future content production toward what the audience is actually searching for.

Ask: what's in the pipeline? Is there flexibility to position upcoming content based on Pinterest research? If yes, the keyword research step becomes even higher-leverage because it shapes content that hasn't been written yet.

**Section Four: The Goals And Success Metrics**

The fourth set of questions defines what success looks like, in measurable terms, on a clear timeline. Without this section, the engagement runs without a clear destination, and at month six everyone disagrees about whether it's working.

**Question 16: What is the primary goal Pinterest needs to deliver?**

Force a single answer. Possible goals include:

Email signups for the free book library.

Direct sales of paid products.

Brand awareness and reach.

Lead generation for service offerings.

Page views for ad revenue.

Community growth (followers on other platforms).

The user might have several goals, but one should be primary. The Pinterest strategy will be optimized for the primary goal first, with secondary goals as bonuses.

For Team Success Network, the natural primary goal is email signups in exchange for free book downloads. Every part of the strategy will be designed around that.

**Question 17: What does success look like at six months, twelve months, and twenty-four months?**

Force specific numbers. Vague goals ("grow Pinterest") produce vague accountability. Specific goals produce clarity.

At six months: how many monthly outbound clicks should Pinterest be driving? How many email signups attributable to Pinterest? How many books downloaded?

At twelve months: same questions, with higher targets reflecting Pinterest's ramp.

At twenty-four months: the steady-state vision of what Pinterest should be contributing to the business.

If the user can't answer these in numbers, help them. Reasonable benchmarks for a content site in a niche like Christian content with the production capacity described might be: 500-2000 monthly outbound clicks at six months, 5000-20000 at twelve months, 20000-50000 at twenty-four months. These are not promises - they're directional benchmarks that can be tightened during the engagement. But naming them explicitly creates a shared expectation that prevents disappointment later.

**Question 18: What's the conversion rate from Pinterest visitor to email signup expected to be?**

Pinterest traffic typically converts to email at 5-15% on well-built landing pages, lower on poorly-built ones. If the user expects 50%, recalibrate them. If they expect 1%, also recalibrate them. The realistic range matters because it determines whether the click volume goals will translate to business outcome goals.

**Question 19: How will success be measured, and how often?**

The analytics-and-iteration file already lays out the measurement framework - weekly check-ins, monthly reviews, quarterly audits. The intake should confirm the user is willing to actually do this measurement. If they're not, the operator needs to know upfront that they'll have to push harder on accountability throughout the engagement.

**Question 20: What would cause the user to stop investing in Pinterest?**

This is the worst-case question, and it's worth asking directly. If outbound clicks aren't growing by month six, what happens? If conversion rates are poor at month nine, what happens? Knowing the user's stop conditions in advance helps calibrate expectations and creates a shared understanding of what "failure" would look like - which often defuses the panic moments that cause premature abandonment.

**Section Five: The Brand And Voice**

The fifth set of questions captures the texture of the brand - its visual identity, its voice, its values. These details inform pin design, copywriting, and overall account aesthetic.

**Question 21: What is the brand's visual identity?**

Ask for: brand colors (with hex codes if available), typography choices, the website's general aesthetic (dark, light, warm, cool, modern, classic), any visual elements that are signature to the brand (logos, motifs, photography styles).

For Team Success Network, the answer might be: dark backgrounds with gold accents, Cinzel for serif headlines and EB Garamond for body, a warm reverent aesthetic with biblical imagery, and a consistent gold-on-dark visual identity across the website. Pin templates should align with this identity.

**Question 22: What is the brand's voice?**

How does the brand sound when it speaks? Pastoral and warm? Direct and instructional? Scholarly and theological? Casual and accessible? Capture three to five adjectives. For Christian content, common voices include: warm and pastoral, biblically grounded, accessible to laypeople, urgent but not alarmist, hopeful, encouraging.

Sample sentences from the website or existing content help calibrate. A brand whose voice is "gentle and pastoral" should not have pins written in aggressive marketing language, and vice versa.

**Question 23: Are there brands or accounts the user admires for their Pinterest presence?**

If the user can name accounts they admire - competitors, adjacent brands, accounts in totally different niches that just feel well-built - those references give the operator concrete examples to study. Look at what those accounts do well: pin design, board structure, posting cadence, copy voice. The user's account doesn't have to copy them, but the references provide useful benchmarks.

**Question 24: Are there things the brand explicitly does not want to be confused with?**

Sometimes the negative space matters as much as the positive. A serious theological library might want to be very clearly distinct from prosperity-gospel content. A Christian leadership brand might want to avoid being lumped in with secular self-help. Capture these explicitly so the keyword research and pin copy don't accidentally drift into off-brand territory.

**Section Six: The Technical And Operational Realities**

The final set of questions covers the infrastructure that supports the Pinterest strategy. Skipping these often means discovering technical blockers months into the engagement, when they're harder to fix.

**Question 25: What's the website built on, and who can edit it?**

WordPress with admin access? Custom-built with developer dependency? A no-code platform with limited flexibility? The answer determines how easily destination pages can be built and optimized. A user with WordPress admin access can make most of the destination page changes themselves. A user dependent on a developer will need to scope and budget for those changes.

For Team Success Network, the user has PHP/MySQL development capacity, so technical edits are feasible - but they take time to schedule and execute. This affects the engagement's pacing.

**Question 26: What email service provider is in use, and what's the existing email funnel like?**

If the primary conversion goal is email signups, the email infrastructure matters. What ESP is in use (Mailchimp, ConvertKit, ActiveCampaign, Beehiiv, custom)? Is there an existing welcome sequence? Is there ongoing email content? A great Pinterest funnel feeding into a non-existent email follow-up is a wasted opportunity.

**Question 27: What design tools are available?**

Canva (free or Pro)? Figma? Photoshop? Templates the user can edit? The pin design file assumes the user has access to a design tool capable of producing 1000x1500 pin templates. If they don't, that's a tool gap to address before pin production begins.

**Question 28: Is there a Pinterest scheduling tool in use, or budget for one?**

Native Pinterest scheduler is free and adequate for most accounts. Tailwind is a paid option for scaling. The intake should establish which the user is starting with, with the understanding that the answer can change as volume grows.

**Question 29: Who else is involved in the Pinterest work?**

Is this the user alone? Is there a team member who handles design? A virtual assistant who does production? A spouse who helps with copy? Knowing the team structure shapes how the workflow gets divided up and what training or documentation needs to be created.

**Question 30: Are there any other constraints, sensitivities, or context the operator should know?**

This is the catch-all question that surfaces things the previous questions didn't. Sensitive past business issues. Family considerations affecting time availability. Regulatory or compliance considerations (rare for content sites, common for some niches). Personal beliefs about specific tactics or platforms. The user might mention things here that meaningfully affect the engagement and that wouldn't have come up otherwise.

**Operator Observations:**

A short paragraph from the operator capturing impressions from the conversation that didn't fit cleanly into the structured questions. This is where the operator notes things like: "User is realistic about the timeline but may need encouragement at month four when traffic is still small," or "Strong content inventory but destinations need significant work before pinning starts," or "Brand voice is well-developed but visual identity needs documentation before pin templates can be aligned."

**Initial Strategic Direction:**

A second short paragraph capturing the operator's preliminary read on the engagement's shape. What's the priority order? What can be deferred? What surprised the operator in the conversation? This becomes the starting point for the keyword research file's setup and the broader engagement plan.

**What To Do With Missing Answers**

Some users won't have answers to every question. That's normal and expected. The operator's job is to distinguish between three types of missing answers.

**Missing-but-can-be-figured-out answers.** The user doesn't know exactly what their audience searches on Pinterest, but the keyword research step will figure it out. Missing answers like this are fine - the operator notes them and proceeds, knowing the answer will emerge during the next step.

**Missing-and-need-to-be-decided answers.** The user hasn't decided what their primary Pinterest goal is. Missing answers like this can't be deferred - they need to be resolved before production begins. The operator pushes for a decision, even a tentative one, and notes that it can be revisited later.

**Missing-and-blocking answers.** The user doesn't have any content yet, or doesn't have a website, or hasn't set up email infrastructure. Missing answers like this mean the engagement isn't ready. The operator should be honest: "Pinterest is a multiplication channel for existing assets. Before we can build a Pinterest strategy, we need to build the assets the strategy will multiply. Let's prioritize that first." This is the moment to be honest about whether to proceed.

In rare cases, the intake reveals that Pinterest isn't a good fit for the user at all. The audience isn't on Pinterest. The content doesn't multiply well. The goals require faster feedback than Pinterest can provide. When this happens, the operator should say so plainly: "Based on what we've discussed, I don't think Pinterest is the right channel for you right now. Here's why, and here's what I'd recommend instead." This is hard but necessary. Building a Pinterest strategy for someone Pinterest can't help is worse than declining the engagement.

**How The Intake Output Feeds The Rest Of The Skill**

Every file that follows depends on the intake's output. Here's the rough mapping.

**The keyword research file** uses the audience description, the audience search phrases, the audience problems, and the secondary audiences to scope the keyword research. The output of the intake's audience section feeds directly into the keyword document the next file produces.

**The profile setup file** uses the brand voice, brand colors, business name, and primary keywords from the audience research to construct the display name, bio, and profile photo treatment.

**The board strategy file** uses the content inventory, the audience analysis, and the keyword document to design the 12-15 board architecture. Without knowing what content exists and what audiences are being served, the boards can't be designed coherently.

**The pin design file** uses the brand visual identity, the brand voice, and the audience aesthetic preferences to build the four to six pin templates. Without these inputs, pin design becomes generic.

**The pin copywriting file** uses the brand voice, the audience problems, and the audience values to calibrate the copy tone across pin titles and descriptions.

**The content multiplication file** uses the content inventory and the production rate to design the multiplication and refresh cadences. A site with 100 pieces of content needs different multiplication math than a site with 10.

**The traffic conversion file** uses the technical infrastructure, the email funnel state, and the goals to design the destination page audits and conversion mechanics. Without knowing what's possible and what's already in place, the conversion work can't be scoped.

**The analytics file** uses the goals, the success metrics, and the measurement cadence to set up the right tracking and review cycles. Without explicit goals from the intake, the analytics framework has nothing to measure against.

**The 90-day launch plan template** (covered later) uses essentially everything from the intake to compile the week-by-week rollout. The launch plan is impossible to write without the intake's outputs.

The intake is, in other words, the upstream input that makes every downstream file possible. Done well, it takes 30 minutes and makes the next 30 weeks of work coherent. Done poorly, it lets fundamental misalignments slip through that surface painfully later. The discipline of the intake is the foundation of the entire engagement.

**What This Means For The Workflow**

By the end of this template, the operator should be able to:

Walk a user through a structured 30-minute intake conversation that captures the necessary inputs for the entire skill.

Push past abstract answers to specifics, in the user's own words.

Identify mismatches early - including the rare cases where Pinterest isn't a fit.

Distinguish between missing answers that can be figured out, missing answers that need to be decided, and missing answers that block the engagement.

Compile the conversation's outputs into a structured intake document that serves as the reference for every other file.

Hand off the intake document to the next step (keyword research) with confidence that the audience, content, goals, brand, and infrastructure are all clearly understood.

The intake is not a formality. It is the foundation. The operator who treats it as the foundation does work that compounds for the next year. The operator who skips it spends the same year discovering, painfully, what they should have known on day one. Take the time. Ask the questions. Capture the answers. Then everything else gets easier.
