**Keyword Research: Finding What Your Audience Actually Searches**

**Why Keyword Research Is The Whole Game**

If the philosophy file made one point, it was this: Pinterest is a search engine. If that's true, then keyword research is not one tactic among many - it is the foundation that everything else sits on. Board names come from keywords. Pin titles come from keywords. Pin descriptions come from keywords. Profile copy comes from keywords. The entire account architecture is a keyword architecture.

Most users skip this step. They guess at what their audience searches, type a few obvious phrases into the bar, and move on. This is the equivalent of writing a website without doing any SEO research and then wondering why nobody finds it. The accounts that win on Pinterest are the ones where the operator did real keyword work upfront and built the entire account on top of that foundation.

The good news: Pinterest keyword research is dramatically easier than Google keyword research. There are no expensive tools required. The platform itself surfaces almost everything you need, for free, in about an hour of focused work. The bad news: most people still don't do it, because it feels less exciting than designing pretty pins. Resist that pull. The keyword list is the most valuable artifact this skill will produce, and it deserves the time.

By the end of this work, the user should have a document containing 30 to 60 keyword phrases, organized by theme, that real Pinterest users are actively typing into the search bar. This document will be referenced for months. Treat it like an asset, not a sketch.

**The Three Layers Of Pinterest Keywords**

Pinterest keywords come in three layers, and a well-built account uses all three.

**Layer one is broad, high-volume keywords.** These are short phrases - usually one to three words - that describe the general category. "Christian books." "Bible verses." "Servant leadership." "Devotionals." High volume, high competition. These are the keywords that go in the profile name, in the bio, and in board names. They establish topical authority but they're hard to rank for individually.

**Layer two is specific, mid-volume keywords.** These are three-to-five-word phrases that narrow the broad category to a specific intent or audience. "Bible verses for anxious mornings." "Christian books for new believers." "Servant leadership in the workplace." "Devotionals for working moms." Medium volume, medium competition. These are the keywords that go in board names, in pin titles, and as the primary keywords in pin descriptions. They're where most of the actual traffic comes from.

**Layer three is long-tail, low-volume keywords.** These are six-to-ten-word phrases that capture a very specific question or scenario. "Bible verses about trusting God in financial hard times." "What does the Bible say about choosing suffering over comfort." "How to start a daily devotional habit as a busy father." Low volume per phrase, but enormous volume in aggregate, and very low competition. These are the phrases that go inside pin descriptions and inside the body of board descriptions. They are also the phrases that Pinterest's algorithm uses to match pins to specific searchers.

A common mistake is to focus only on layer one. Users feel like they need to "rank for" big terms like "Christian books," and they pour all their effort there. But layer one is brutally competitive - accounts with hundreds of thousands of monthly viewers are fighting for those terms. The user is not going to win there in their first year, and they don't need to. The actual traffic, especially in the first 12 months, comes from layers two and three. A pin that ranks number one for "Bible verses for anxious mornings" will drive far more traffic than a pin that ranks number 47 for "Bible verses."

The strategic takeaway is that the keyword document should be heavy in layers two and three. A good distribution is roughly five to ten layer-one keywords (used for structure and authority), 20 to 30 layer-two keywords (used for boards and pin titles), and 30 to 60 layer-three keywords (used inside descriptions and as pin angles). The pyramid widens as it goes down.

**Method One: The Pinterest Search Bar**

The single most powerful keyword research tool on Pinterest is the search bar itself. It does the thing that paid SEO tools charge hundreds of dollars to do: it tells you exactly what real users are typing in, in real time, with no extra step required.

Here is how to use it.

Open Pinterest. Click into the search bar. Type a single broad keyword from your niche - let's say "Christian books." Do not press enter. Just look at what Pinterest auto-suggests beneath the bar.

What you'll see is a dropdown of phrases that real Pinterest users are actively searching, sorted roughly by volume. For "Christian books," that dropdown might show: _Christian books to read, Christian books for women, Christian books for men, Christian books for teens, Christian books for new believers, Christian books for marriage, Christian books for moms, Christian books fiction._

Every single one of those phrases is a keyword someone is searching right now. Copy them all into a document. That's eight layer-two keywords from one search bar query, and you've spent thirty seconds.

Now type one of those phrases - say "Christian books for new believers" - and again look at the autocomplete. You'll see further refinements: _Christian books for new believers women, Christian books for new believers free, Christian books for new believers reading list, Christian books for new believers basics._ Now you have layer-three keywords. Copy them all.

Repeat this process for every layer-one keyword in the niche. For a Christian library, that means doing this exercise for "Christian books," "Bible verses," "devotionals," "biblical leadership," "Christian living," "scripture," "faith," "Christian quotes," "prayer," and any other obvious entry points. Each of those will branch into ten to fifteen sub-phrases. Each sub-phrase will branch into another five to ten.

In about 45 minutes of this work, you will have a document with 100 to 200 raw keyword phrases, all of them confirmed as actual searches by real Pinterest users. This is dramatically more valuable than any list of keywords pulled from a generic SEO tool, because Pinterest's autocomplete reflects Pinterest's actual user base - which skews heavily female, planning-oriented, and intent-driven, and which searches differently than Google users do.

A few tips for getting the most out of this method:

**Search on mobile and desktop both.** The autocomplete results are usually similar but occasionally differ, and the differences can surface useful keywords.

**Search while logged out, in an incognito window, occasionally.** Pinterest personalizes autocomplete based on your activity. Logged out, you see the platform's general autocomplete, which is closer to what a fresh user would see. This matters because your audience is mostly fresh users, not power users with Pinterest histories like yours.

**Pay attention to surprising suggestions.** If you type "Christian books" and the autocomplete suggests "Christian books for moms going through divorce," that's a window into a specific, painful, high-intent search that you might never have thought of on your own. These surprise suggestions are gold. They reveal real questions real people are typing in moments of need.

**Don't filter prematurely.** While doing autocomplete research, copy every reasonable suggestion. Don't try to evaluate which ones to keep yet. The filtering happens later, after you have a complete picture of the search landscape.

**Method Two: The Guided Search Tiles**

After you press enter on a Pinterest search, the platform shows you results - but it also shows a row of tiles directly above the results, often labeled with related search terms. These tiles are Pinterest's own categorization of how the broad keyword breaks down into sub-topics.

For example, search "Christian living" and the tiles above the results might say: _quotes, women, scripture, marriage, encouragement, men, family, books._ Each tile is clickable, and clicking it both narrows the search and reveals further tiles.

These tiles are different from autocomplete. Autocomplete shows you what users type. Tiles show you how Pinterest itself categorizes the topic - its taxonomy, basically. Both are valuable, and they don't fully overlap.

Spend ten or fifteen minutes clicking through tiles for each of your layer-one keywords. Capture every tile label. Capture every sub-tile that appears after you click in. By the end of this exercise, you'll have a structural map of how Pinterest organizes your niche, which directly informs how you should organize your boards. If Pinterest's tiles for "Christian living" include "marriage," "family," "encouragement," and "quotes," those are signals that boards organized around those exact themes will fit naturally into the platform's existing structure.

The tiles are also useful as a sanity check. If a tile you expected doesn't appear, that might mean the topic is less searched than you assumed. If a tile you didn't expect does appear, that's a topic you should consider creating content around.

**Method Three: Pinterest Trends**

Pinterest Trends is a free tool, separate from the main app, available at trends.pinterest.com. Most Pinterest users don't know it exists. It is one of the most useful tools on the entire platform.

Trends does three things:

It shows search volume over time for any keyword you enter, going back several years. You can see whether a topic is growing, declining, or seasonal. For evergreen content, you want growing or stable. For seasonal content, you want to see the seasonal pattern clearly so you can pin ahead of the spike.

It shows related keywords that are trending alongside your seed keyword, often surfacing rising terms before they show up in autocomplete.

It shows top pins for a keyword, which lets you see what's actually performing on that search term. This is enormously useful for design inspiration and angle research - but more on that in a moment.

Here is the workflow:

Open Trends. Type in a layer-one keyword from your niche. Look at the trend line. Is the topic growing, flat, or declining? If it's declining sharply, that's a signal to deprioritize it. If it's growing, that's a signal to invest more there.

Look at the seasonality. Almost every Pinterest topic has some seasonality. "Christian books" spikes in January (new year, fresh starts) and again in late summer (back-to-school, fall reset). Devotionals spike at the start of every quarter. Marriage content spikes in spring (wedding season). Knowing the seasonal pattern lets you pin three to six weeks ahead of the spike, so your pins are already indexed and ranking when the search volume rises.

Look at the related keywords. Trends often surfaces phrases that haven't yet hit autocomplete because they're growing too fast. These are early-mover opportunities. If you can be one of the first accounts to publish quality pins on a rising keyword, you'll lock in ranking position that's very hard to dislodge later.

Look at the top pins. Click into them. What do they look like? What's the title format? What's the visual style? What's the destination URL? You're not copying - you're studying the search engine's revealed preference. If every top pin for a keyword is a pastel background with a script font and a short list-style title, that's the format the algorithm is currently rewarding for that keyword. You should produce something in that family while still differentiating.

Trends has one limitation worth noting: data quality varies by topic and country. Niche topics sometimes show "not enough data," especially for layer-three phrases. When this happens, fall back to autocomplete and tile research. Trends is best for layers one and two.

**Method Four: Studying Top Pins And Top Accounts**

After you've done autocomplete, tiles, and Trends, do one more pass: study the top pins and top accounts for your most important keywords.

Search a layer-two keyword. Look at the first 20 pins that appear. For each pin, ask:

What is the title? What language pattern does it use? Is it a list ("7 Christian Books for New Believers"), a how-to ("How to Build a Daily Devotional Habit"), a question ("Are You Putting God First?"), or a promise ("The Devotional That Will Change Your Mornings")?

What is the visual format? Light or dark? Photo or graphic? Bold sans-serif or elegant serif? Single column of text or layered design?

Where does it link to? Click through. Is the destination page well-built or thin? If the top-ranking pins are all linking to mediocre pages, that's an opportunity - your better page can outrank them once you build comparable pin authority.

Who is the account? Click their profile. How many pins do they have? What other boards do they run? What's their display name and bio doing? You're reverse-engineering the operators who have already won the keyword you want to win.

This is not about copying. It's about understanding the implicit rules of each keyword's ranking - the format, the angle, the visual style, the destination quality. Every keyword has its own micro-game, and the top pins reveal what that game looks like right now.

A specific tactic that works well: take a layer-two keyword you want to rank for, find the top three pins, and write down what they have in common and what's missing from all of them. The "what's missing" is your angle. If every top pin for "devotionals for working moms" is a list of devotional book recommendations, but none of them address "how to find time to actually read the devotional," that gap is your opening. Pin into the gap.

**Organizing The Keyword Document**

By now, the user has somewhere between 100 and 300 raw keyword phrases collected from autocomplete, tiles, Trends, and competitor research. The next step is to turn this raw list into a working document.

Create a simple structured document - a spreadsheet works well, but a plain text or markdown document is fine too. Organize it like this:

**Section one: Layer-one keywords.** Five to ten broad terms that define the niche. These will inform the profile name, bio, and the broadest board categories. For Team Success Network: _Christian books, devotionals, biblical leadership, Christian living, scripture, prayer, faith._

**Section two: Layer-two keywords, grouped by theme.** Twenty to forty mid-tail phrases organized into thematic clusters. Each cluster should map roughly to a board you'll later create. So for the cluster "biblical leadership," the layer-two keywords might be: _biblical leadership principles, biblical leadership for pastors, biblical leadership in business, servant leadership Bible, Christian leadership development, leading with humility, biblical leadership for men._

**Section three: Layer-three keywords, attached to specific content angles.** Thirty to sixty long-tail phrases, each tagged to either an existing piece of content on the website or a content angle the user could create. These become individual pin angles. For example, the layer-three phrase "Bible verses about choosing suffering over comfort" maps directly to the user's existing book on Jesus choosing the crown of thorns - and becomes a pin title.

**Section four: Seasonal calendar.** Note any keywords that show clear seasonality from Trends research. This becomes a calendar - "Pin January-spike content starting mid-November," "Pin Easter content starting late February," and so on.

This document is the user's keyword bible for the next 12 months. Refer to it every time you write a board name, pin title, or description. Refresh it once a year by re-running the autocomplete and Trends research, since search behavior shifts over time. But do not refresh it more often than that - the keyword landscape doesn't change fast enough to justify constant re-research, and the time is better spent producing pins.

**Filtering: Which Keywords To Actually Use**

Not every keyword that exists is worth targeting. Some have too much competition for a new account to crack. Some are technically searched but don't match the user's actual content. Some are searched mostly by people who will never become website visitors.

A useful filtering process:

**Cut keywords that are obviously off-topic.** If autocomplete suggested "Christian books for kids" but the website has zero kids' content and no plans to add any, drop it. There's no value in ranking for searches you can't satisfy.

**Cut keywords that misalign with the audience.** "Christian gifts" is a searched term, but if the website doesn't sell or recommend gifts, ranking for that keyword would just send unqualified traffic that bounces. Bounce rates feed back into Pinterest's understanding of your account.

**Keep keywords that match the existing or planned content inventory.** A useful exercise is to take each layer-two keyword and ask: "Which page on the website would I send this pin to?" If you can name the page, keep the keyword. If you can't, either deprioritize it or note it as a content idea for future production.

**Keep keywords with intent that matches the goal.** If the goal is email signups, keywords with informational intent ("Bible verses for anxious mornings") work well, because the searcher is in problem-solving mode and the pin can offer a free resource. If the goal is book sales or downloads, keywords with discovery intent ("best Christian books to read") work well. If the goal is brand awareness, broader keywords are fine. Match the keyword's implicit intent to the conversion goal of the destination page.

After filtering, the user should have a leaner, sharper list - maybe 50 to 80 keywords total - that they're confident in pursuing. This becomes the working list. Anything cut can be archived for possible future use.

**A Worked Example For A Christian Library**

To make this concrete, here's how the keyword research process looks for Team Success Network specifically.

Layer-one keywords, established from the niche: _Christian books, devotionals, biblical leadership, Christian living, scripture, prayer, faith, servant leadership, Christian quotes._

After autocomplete research on "Christian books" alone, layer-two keywords surface: _Christian books for women, Christian books for men, Christian books for new believers, Christian books to read in a crisis, free Christian books, Christian books on suffering, Christian books on identity, Christian books on leadership, Christian books for pastors._

After autocomplete on "devotionals," more layer-two keywords surface: _daily devotionals for women, morning devotionals, devotionals for couples, devotionals for new Christians, devotionals for working moms, devotionals for men, short daily devotionals, devotionals on God's promises._

Trends research reveals that "Christian books" peaks in January and August, "devotionals" peaks at the start of every quarter, and "biblical leadership" is a slowly growing year-round term with no major seasonality.

Layer-three phrases attached to specific content: For the user's book on Jesus choosing suffering over comfort, layer-three phrases like _what does it mean to take up your cross daily, why did Jesus choose the crown of thorns, Bible verses about choosing pain over comfort, scriptures on dying to self._ For the user's book on seeking God's kingdom first, phrases like _what does seek first the kingdom of God mean, how to put God first in daily life, scriptures on putting God first, Christian books on God's priorities._

Filtering: Drop "Christian books for kids" (no inventory), "Christian gifts" (no inventory), "Christian fiction" (off-brand for the library). Keep everything else.

Final working list: roughly 60 keywords across three layers, organized by theme, ready to drive board structure and pin production.

This is the kind of document that takes about three focused hours to build the first time and then drives the next year of Pinterest work. The investment is real but the return is enormous: every board the user creates will have its name and description pulled directly from this document, every pin title will pull from layer-two phrases, every pin description will weave in layer-three phrases, and the entire account will hum with consistent, indexable, search-aligned signals.

**What To Do With This List Next**

Once the keyword document is complete, the workflow proceeds in this order:

The layer-one keywords feed into the profile setup - display name, bio, and the structural backbone of the board library.

The layer-two keywords feed into board names and board descriptions. Each board takes its name from a layer-two phrase and its description from a paragraph that weaves in three to five related layer-two and layer-three phrases.

The layer-three keywords feed into pin titles, pin descriptions, and content angles. Each pin's title is built around one layer-three phrase, and the description is built around the phrase plus two or three semantically related ones.

Every other reference file in this skill assumes this keyword document exists. Without it, board structure is guesswork, pin titles are guesswork, descriptions are guesswork, and the entire account drifts. With it, every decision becomes mechanical: "What phrase from the document does this pin target? Use it in the title. Use the related phrases in the description. Move on."

The keyword document is the closest thing this skill has to a single source of truth. Build it carefully, refer to it constantly, and refresh it once a year. The accounts that win on Pinterest are the ones whose operators treat the keyword document the way a chef treats their mise en place - set up before the cooking starts, kept clean throughout, and never neglected.
